# Costs and minimums

Costs and minimums differ between ETFs and index mutual funds, though for broad index products the gap on expense ratios has largely closed. The expense ratio is the annual percentage a fund charges to run itself. Both forms can carry very low expense ratios, but ETFs add a trading spread, while mutual funds may impose a minimum initial investment.

## Expense ratios

The expense ratio is the ongoing yearly cost, expressed as a percentage of your investment, deducted automatically from the fund. For broad, popular index funds, both ETFs and mutual funds now offer very low expense ratios, often a small fraction of a percent. This is the cost that matters most over the long run because it is paid every year. When comparing two funds tracking the same index, the expense ratio is usually the first number to check, and the two forms are often close.

## Spreads and commissions

ETFs trade like stocks, so they have a bid-ask spread: a small gap between the price to buy and the price to sell. Crossing that spread is a tiny cost each time you trade, larger for thinly traded ETFs and negligible for popular ones. Many brokers now charge no commission on ETF trades, but the spread remains. Index mutual funds have no spread, since they transact at net asset value, though some fund families charge fees if bought outside their own platform.

## Minimums

Index mutual funds often require a minimum initial investment, which can be a few thousand dollars, though many have low or no minimums depending on the provider and account type. An ETF, by contrast, can be bought for the price of a single share, and with fractional shares now common at many brokers, even less. This makes ETFs easy to start small with. The cost and minimum picture favors neither form universally; it depends on the specific funds, the broker, and how you invest.
