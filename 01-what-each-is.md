# What each is

An ETF and an index mutual fund are both pooled funds that hold a basket of investments on behalf of many investors, and both commonly track an index. The difference is in their legal and operational wrapper: an ETF is structured to trade on a stock exchange like a share, while a mutual fund is bought from and sold back to the fund company. Underneath, an index version of either simply holds the securities in a chosen index.

## What an index fund tracks

Both forms are often used to track an index, which is a defined list of holdings such as a broad market benchmark. An index fund does not try to beat the market by picking winners; it aims to match the index by holding what the index holds in the same proportions. This passive approach keeps costs low because there is little research or active trading. The same index can be tracked by an ETF and by a mutual fund offered side by side.

## How an ETF is structured

An ETF issues shares that trade on an exchange. Large institutions called authorized participants can create new ETF shares by delivering the underlying basket of securities to the fund, or redeem shares by taking the basket back. This creation and redemption mechanism keeps the ETF's market price close to the value of its holdings and, importantly, lets the fund move securities in and out without selling them for cash. That mechanism underlies several of the ETF's later advantages.

## How an index mutual fund is structured

An index mutual fund issues and redeems its shares directly with investors, not on an exchange. When you buy, the fund creates shares for you; when you sell, the fund buys them back, both at the net asset value calculated after the market closes. To meet redemptions, the fund may need to sell holdings for cash. This direct relationship between investor and fund, settled once per day at one price, is the defining structural trait that separates it from an ETF.
