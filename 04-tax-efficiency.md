# Tax efficiency

ETFs are generally more tax-efficient than index mutual funds in taxable accounts, mainly because of how their shares are created and redeemed. The difference centers on capital gains distributions: amounts a fund must pass to shareholders, who then owe tax on them, when the fund sells holdings at a profit. ETFs tend to generate fewer of these distributions than comparable mutual funds.

## Why distributions happen

When a fund sells a holding for more than it paid, it realizes a capital gain. By law, funds must distribute net realized gains to shareholders, who owe tax on them in a taxable account, even if they never sold a share themselves. A mutual fund that has to sell holdings to meet investor redemptions, or to rebalance, can realize gains that get passed along. This can create a tax bill in a year when you simply held the fund.

## The ETF advantage

The ETF creation and redemption mechanism lets the fund hand baskets of securities to authorized participants rather than selling them for cash. Because this in-kind transfer is not a taxable sale for the fund, ETFs can meet outflows and reshuffle holdings while realizing far fewer capital gains. The result is that broad index ETFs often distribute little or no capital gains in a typical year, leaving the investor to control timing by choosing when to sell their own shares.

## Where it matters and where it does not

Tax efficiency matters in taxable brokerage accounts, where distributions create real tax bills. It matters much less, or not at all, in tax-advantaged accounts like retirement accounts, where gains are sheltered and distributions are not taxed as they occur. So for an investor holding inside a retirement account, the tax difference between an ETF and an index mutual fund largely disappears. The advantage is real but situational, and this is a general explanation rather than tax advice for any individual.
